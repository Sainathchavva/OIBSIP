Srinivasa Ramanujan

A self-taught genius who found infinity in a school notebook.

Who He Was

Srinivasa Ramanujan was born in 1887 in the town of Erode in South India and grew up in modest circumstances in Kumbakonam. He had almost no access to formal mathematical training beyond school textbooks, yet by his teenage years he was already filling notebooks with original results in number theory, infinite series, and continued fractions — much of it worked out entirely on his own, using little more than slate, chalk, and an old book of five thousand theorems he had found as a boy.

Struggling to find institutional support in India, Ramanujan began writing letters to mathematicians in England describing his findings. Most did not reply, but G. H. Hardy at Cambridge University recognized that the strange, unproven formulas in front of him could only have come from someone with real mathematical insight. Hardy later said that instinct alone told him the results "must be true, because if they were not true, no one would have had the imagination to invent them."

Ramanujan travelled to England in 1914 to work with Hardy, despite the difficulties of leaving home, adapting to an unfamiliar climate and diet, and working within a mathematical tradition very different from the one he had built for himself in isolation. Over the next few years the two collaborated closely, and Ramanujan's work touched areas including partition theory, modular forms, and infinite series for numbers like pi — several of which are still studied and applied in modern mathematics and physics.

His health declined during his time in England, and he returned to India in 1919, passing away the following year at just 32. In that short life he produced roughly 3,900 results, many of which were unproven when he wrote them down but have since been confirmed true. His notebooks continue to be studied by mathematicians today, and his story is often used as an example of how far raw mathematical intuition can travel with almost no formal support.

Key Milestones

1887

Born in Erode, Tamil Nadu, India.

1903

Discovered a book of mathematical theorems and began independent research on his own.

1913

Wrote to G. H. Hardy at Cambridge with pages of original results, leading to his discovery by the mathematical world.

1914–1919

Worked with Hardy at Cambridge, publishing groundbreaking work on partitions, series, and number theory.

1918

Elected a Fellow of the Royal Society, one of the youngest members at the time.

1920

Passed away in India at age 32, leaving behind notebooks still studied by mathematicians today.
